# ISO 14000 (Environmental Management System)

## 1. Overview

### A. Definition
> A family of international standards concerning the **Environmental Management System (EMS)** by which an organization systematically manages and improves its impact on the environment; the core is **ISO 14001** (EMS requirements), the standard subject to certification.

The key point of ISO 14000 is that it is not a "performance standard" that directly prescribes emission limits for specific pollutants, but a **"system standard" that requires an organization to set its own environmental goals and equip itself with a management system (process) to achieve them**. That is, it certifies not "how clean it is" but "whether a management structure that improves the environment operates." For this reason, it can be applied regardless of industry or size and has continuous improvement (PDCA) built in.

### B. Background and Necessity
Since the 1990s, as the climate crisis and environmental regulations strengthened and stakeholders such as consumers, investors, and business partners demanded corporate environmental responsibility, the need grew to bundle the environmental-management methods each country and company used separately into an **internationally unified standard**. ISO extended the successful model of its quality management standard (ISO 9000) into the environmental field, enacting ISO 14001 in 1996. Today, as **ESG and sustainability management** have become conditions for corporate survival, ISO 14001 has established itself as a practical management tool that manages environmental risk, preemptively responds to regulation, and lowers costs through resource and energy efficiency. In particular, since large companies often require certification of their suppliers in the global supply chain, it has become a **de facto entry requirement for exports and transactions**.

## 2. Certification Standards (Major)

ISO 14000 is not a single standard but a bundle of purpose-specific standards. The object of certification (third-party review) is **ISO 14001 alone**, and the rest are guideline and method standards that support it.

- **ISO 14001**: The certification standard prescribing the requirements an EMS must have. It follows the structure of leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, and improvement (HLS).
- **ISO 14004**: A practical guideline (guide) for actually building and operating ISO 14001.
- **ISO 14040/44**: The **Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)** method that quantifies environmental impact from raw-material extraction to disposal of a product.
- **ISO 14064**: A standard for **calculating, reporting, and verifying greenhouse gas emissions** of an organization or project, serving as the basis for carbon-neutrality and emissions-trading responses.

| Standard | Nature | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **ISO 14001** | Certification requirements | EMS requirements (object of third-party certification) |
| **ISO 14004** | Guideline | EMS construction/operation guide |
| **ISO 14040/44** | Method | Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) |
| **ISO 14064** | Method | Greenhouse gas calculation/verification |

## 3. Construction and Certification Procedure

The backbone of ISO 14001 is the **PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act) cycle**. The idea that environmental management does not end with a single measure but continuously improves performance by repeating the loop of setting goals and executing, checking, and improving is reflected directly in the standard's structure.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  P[Establish environmental policy] --> D[Build EMS<br/>PDCA]
  D --> A[Internal audit]
  A --> C[Certification review<br/>stage 1·2]
  C --> R[Certificate issuance · surveillance]
```

In the Plan (P) stage, top management declares an environmental policy, identifies the **environmental aspects** by which the organization's activities affect the environment and the laws to be complied with, and sets goals. In the Do (D) stage, it allocates resources and roles, controls operations, and equips emergency-response procedures. In the Check (C) stage, it monitors performance and finds and corrects nonconformities through internal audits. In the Act (A) stage, it re-evaluates the overall system through management review and raises the goals for the next cycle. Thereafter, an external certification body confirms the completeness of the system in **stage 1 (document review)** and actual implementation in **stage 2 (on-site review)**, issues the certificate, and typically maintains validity with an annual surveillance review and a 3-year renewal review.

| Stage | Content |
|---|---|
| **Plan (P)** | Environmental policy, identify environmental aspects/laws, set goals |
| **Do (D)** | Allocate resources/roles, control operations, emergency response |
| **Check (C)** | Monitoring, internal audit, nonconformity correction |
| **Act (A)** | Management review, continuous improvement |
| **Certification** | Stage 1 (document), stage 2 (on-site) review → issuance, surveillance/renewal review |

## 4. Certification Effects

Certification effects have both a defensive side of regulatory response and an offensive side of competitiveness and cost. For example, when a manufacturing company introduces an EMS and pursues waste separation/recycling and energy efficiency, waste-treatment and electricity costs are actually reduced, so environmental performance and cost savings are often achieved simultaneously.

| Effect | Content |
|---|---|
| **Regulatory compliance** | Preemptive response to environmental regulation, reduced violation risk |
| **Cost savings** | Cost improvement through energy/resource/waste reduction |
| **Trust/competitiveness** | Image enhancement, meeting export/transaction requirements |
| **ESG** | Objective basis for sustainability management and disclosure |

## 5. Considerations and Implications
From a professional engineer's perspective, the thing to guard against most is viewing certification as **an end rather than a means**. Substantive environmental performance and continuous improvement are the essence rather than obtaining the certificate itself, and a certification equipped only with formal documents actually harms trust. First, operating ISO 14001 in integration (IMS) with **ISO 50001 (energy management) and ISO 9001 (quality)** can reduce duplicate reviews and documents and create synergy. Second, in the trend of mandatory ESG disclosure, ISO 14064 (greenhouse gas) and LCA data must be linked as disclosure evidence. Third, to prevent **greenwashing**, which inflates environmental performance through estimation or exaggeration rather than actual measurement, securing the reliability of data measurement and verification becomes increasingly important.

---

> **In one line**: ISO 14000 is a *family of international standards for the Environmental Management System (EMS)* that, centered on ISO 14001, the object of certification, goes through PDCA-based construction and stage-1/stage-2 review, providing regulatory compliance, cost savings, and ESG competitiveness; the key is substantive environmental performance, integrated operation, and prevention of greenwashing rather than formal certification.
